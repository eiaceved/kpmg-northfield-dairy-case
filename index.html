01

How a KPMG Deal Advisory M&A case works

When a company buys a rival, it usually pays more than the rival is worth on its own. That extra is the premium. Deal Advisory teams help the buyer check whether the savings and growth from combining, called synergies, are worth more than the premium.

Put simply: what are we paying extra, what do we get for it, and how sure are we of getting it? Formats vary by office and team, so confirm the details with your recruiter.

How the interview runs

Based on public prep guides

  • Cases are usually candidate-led: you set the structure and ask for data.
  • M&A cases are common at KPMG, often with synergy sizing and simple valuation.
  • Later rounds can include a written case: a packet of charts and tables you turn into a short presentation.
  • Expect to defend your recommendation when the interviewer pushes back.

What the buyer needs

The four questions

  • Does the deal make strategic sense?
  • What's the target worth on its own, and what's the premium?
  • How big are the synergies, and how likely?
  • What will integration cost, and what could go wrong?

The synergy test

  1. RationaleWhy combine?
  2. PremiumWhat's the extra cost?
  3. SynergiesWhat's the extra value?
  4. IntegrationCan we actually get it?

The deal creates value only if synergies, after costs, beat the premium

Ten terms to drop naturally

Standalone value

What the target is worth on its own, often EBITDA times the multiple similar companies trade at.

Premium

The amount paid above standalone value. Paying 416 million for a company worth 320 million is a 30 percent premium.

Cost synergies

Savings from combining, like closing an extra plant or buying supplies in bigger volumes. Usually the most reliable kind.

Revenue synergies

Extra sales from combining, like selling one company's products to the other's customers. Usually less certain.

Dis-synergies

Value lost by combining, like customers who leave because they don't want both brands from one supplier.

Run-rate

The yearly level synergies reach once fully in place, often after two or three years.

Integration costs

One-time costs to capture synergies, like severance, plant closure, and combining IT systems.

Risk-adjusted synergies

Synergies discounted for how often deals actually deliver them.

Clean team

A small group allowed to see a rival's sensitive data before closing, so the companies stay within competition rules.

Integration management office

The team that runs the integration and tracks whether each synergy is being delivered.

Your move

Restate the question in one sentence, then say what you'd like to clarify.

Strong answer

"So Northfield is being asked to pay about 96 million above Bluewater's standalone value, and the board wants to know whether the synergies justify that, and what integration must deliver. Is that right?"

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • Why does Northfield want Bluewater?
    Milk demand is shrinking, and both companies have empty plant capacity. Combining would let them run fewer, fuller plants.
  • Has anyone estimated synergies?
    Management estimates about 20 million dollars a year by year three. We'll show you the breakdown.
  • What will integration cost?
    About 30 million dollars, one time: closing a plant, severance, and combining IT systems.
  • Any regulatory concerns?
    Competition authorities will review the deal, but lawyers expect approval because many rivals remain.
  • How has Northfield done with past deals?
    It hasn't done a deal this size before.

The last answer matters: a first big integration raises the risk of missing synergy targets.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

How would you approach this?

Your move

Take 60 seconds to sketch your structure. Then walk through it, say where you'd start, and ask for data.

Strong framework

Do the synergies justify a 30% premium for Bluewater, and what must integration deliver?
1Strategic rationale
  • Market trends: is demand shrinking?
  • Overlap: plants, regions, customers
  • Fit: products and brands
2Value and premium
  • Standalone value: EBITDA × multiple
  • Premium in dollars
  • Any other bidders?
3Synergies
  • Cost: plants, buying, routes, back office
  • Revenue: cross-selling
  • Dis-synergies: lost customers
4Integration and risks
  • One-time costs and timing
  • How likely each synergy is
  • Approvals and people

Say it like this

"The deal only creates value if synergies, after integration costs, are worth more than the premium, so I'd structure it around that test. First, the strategic rationale: why combine, and how much the two companies overlap. Second, the premium: Bluewater is worth about 320 million on its own, so we'd pay about 96 million extra. Third, the synergies: cost, revenue, and any lost customers, and how certain each is. Fourth, integration: what it costs, how long it takes, and what could go wrong. My hypothesis is that cost synergies from closing a plant carry this deal, so I'd like to start with the market and plant data."

Why it works

  • It states the decision rule up front: synergies after costs must beat the premium.
  • It separates cost from revenue synergies, which have very different odds.
  • It puts a dollar figure on the premium immediately.

If you blank, "rationale, premium, synergies, integration" works for almost any acquisition case where the buyer is a company, not a fund.

05

Exhibit 1: the market and the two companies

Interviewer

Here's six years of market and company data. What stands out?

Which finding should you lead with?

Milk drinking per person fell about 10 percent in five years. Northfield's plants dropped from 80 to 70 percent full, and Bluewater's from 75 to 60. Store brands keep taking share, which holds prices down, and milk costs have climbed. With flat margins and empty capacity, the obvious move is to run fewer, fuller plants and buy milk at greater scale. That's cost synergy, not growth.

Then

Say the insight, tie it to the deal, and ask for the next piece of data.

Say it like this

"This is a consolidation deal. Demand is shrinking, both companies run plants well below capacity, and store brands keep prices low, so neither company can grow its way out. Combining lets them run fewer, fuller plants and buy milk at scale. That tells me cost synergies should carry the deal. Can I see the synergy estimates by source?"

Notice the pattern: insight, then so-what for the deal, then your own next ask.

06

Exhibit 2: management's synergy estimates

Interviewer

Here's management's estimate of yearly synergies once fully in place, by year three. What's your read?

What's the so-what?

Cost synergies add up to 18 million: 8 from closing a plant, 4 from buying together, 3 from shared delivery routes, and 3 from one back office. Cross-selling adds 3, minus 1 for lost customers, for a net of about 20. History says buyers get about 85 percent of cost synergies but only 40 percent of revenue ones, so nearly all the value rests on cost, and especially on the plant closure.

Then

Synthesize both exhibits in three sentences.

Say it like this

"This is a consolidation deal in a shrinking market, and the synergies match that: about 20 million a year net, 18 of it from costs, mostly the plant closure. Cost synergies are the reliable kind, but revenue synergies usually come in well below plan. Next I'd value the synergies and compare them with the 96 million premium, both on plan and on a realistic delivery rate."

Do the synergies pay for the premium?

The core math of an M&A case: value the synergies, subtract integration costs, and compare with the premium. Then ask what happens if the plan isn't fully delivered.

Your move

Say the test first: synergy value minus integration costs must beat the premium. Do it on plan, then on a realistic delivery rate.

  1. 1 · The premium$416M − ($40M × 8 = $320M)$96M
  2. 2 · Net synergies, on plan$18M + $3M − $1M a year$20M
  3. 3 · Value, on plan$20M × 8 = $160M, − $30M integration$130M
  4. 4 · On plan$130M − $96M premium+$34M
  5. 5 · Break-even synergies($96M + $30M) ÷ 8 ≈ $16M a year = 80% of plan≈$16M
  6. 6 · Risk-adjusted($15.3M + $1.2M − $1M) × 8 − $30M. Just below $96M≈$94M

Quick check: what yearly synergies does Northfield need just to break even?

The premium of 96 million plus 30 million of integration costs is 126 million. At 8 times, that takes about 16 million a year, 80 percent of the plan. Everyday version: if you pay 1,260 dollars extra for a car that saves you 100 dollars a year in gas, and a year of savings is worth 8 times itself, you need about 160 dollars a year of savings to break even.

Then

Summarize the math in two sentences.

Say it like this

"On plan, synergies are worth about 130 million after integration costs, which beats the 96 million premium by about 34 million. But the deal breaks even only if Northfield delivers about 80 percent of the plan, and at typical delivery rates the synergies are worth about 94 million, just under the premium. So the 30 percent premium leaves no cushion."

If the interviewer asks, "What premium would you accept?": "About 25 percent, or 80 million. That leaves about 14 million of cushion on the risk-adjusted synergies, and it keeps some of the value for Northfield's shareholders instead of handing it all to the seller."

Score the integration priorities

Interviewer

Say the deal goes ahead. Here are six integration moves. Score each for ease and impact. Which three should the integration office start with?

Your move

Score each move out loud with a one-line reason, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd start with three. Combine purchasing, because it's quick and worth about 4 million. Set up joint account teams for the top customers from day one, because losing customers is the fastest way to destroy value. And plan the plant closure early, because it's the single biggest synergy and takes the longest. Cross-selling can wait until the basics are working."

    Which three come first?

    A is quick money. B protects against the dis-synergy that could sink the deal. C is the biggest synergy and needs the longest lead time. D and E matter but are smaller or slower, and F is the least reliable synergy, so it comes later.

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

    Strong answer

    Answer3 reasonsRiskNext steps

    "Northfield should buy Bluewater, but at a premium of no more than about 25 percent, around 400 million dollars, not 416, and it should run the integration around cost synergies."

    Bonus drills: sizing and quick deal math

    Short reps for the math that shows up in almost every M&A case.

    Market sizing: milk in one metro

    Interviewer

    How many gallons of milk does a metro area of 1 million people drink in a year?

    Your move

    Start with people, narrow to milk drinkers, then multiply by how much they drink. Say each assumption out loud.

    1. Milk drinkers1M × 70%700K
    2. Per person1 cup a day = 1/16 gallon1/16 gal
    3. Per day700K ÷ 16≈44K gal
    4. Per year44K × 365≈16M gal
    5. Store value16M × $4≈$64M
    6. So what2% decline ≈ 300K fewer gallons a year. That's the empty capacity✓

    All inputs are interview-style assumptions, not market research.

    Quick deal math

    1. A company worth 200 million is bought for 250 million. What's the premium?

    "50 million, which is 25 percent of 200."

    2. Synergies of 10 million a year, valued at 8 times. What are they worth?

    "80 million, before subtracting integration costs."

    3. A 60 million premium plus 20 million of integration costs, at 8 times. What yearly synergies break even?

    "80 million divided by 8 is 10 million a year."

    4. Why do buyers discount revenue synergies more than cost synergies?

    "Cost synergies are mostly in the buyer's control, like closing a plant. Revenue synergies depend on customers buying more and competitors not responding, so they often come in well below plan."
